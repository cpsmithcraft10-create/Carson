# Does HGH create new muscle cells?

**Short answer: No — not in humans, on the evidence available.** Growth hormone (GH/HGH)
does not add new muscle *fibers*, and in healthy adults it does not even reliably add
contractile muscle protein to existing fibers. Most of the "lean mass" it adds is water
and connective tissue. The cell-level claims that circulate in bodybuilding circles come
from cell-culture and rodent work that has not replicated in people.

*Compiled 2026-09-19. Informational summary of published research, not medical advice.*

---

## 1. Three questions people mix together

"New muscle cells" can mean three different things. They have three different answers:

| Claim | What it means | Verdict in humans |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperplasia** | The muscle contains *more fibers* than before | No evidence — from GH or anything else |
| **Myonuclear accretion** | Satellite cells (muscle stem cells) fuse into existing fibers, donating nuclei | Real biology, but GH's role in humans is unproven; one GH trial found hypertrophy *without* added nuclei |
| **Hypertrophy** | Existing fibers get bigger | Happens with training; GH adds little or nothing in healthy adults |

The bodybuilding claim "HGH causes hyperplasia, so it gives you permanent new muscle"
conflates all three.

---

## 2. Human muscle fiber number looks fixed

The foundational human data predate the GH question and apply to it directly.

- **MacDougall et al. (1984), *J Appl Physiol* 57(5):1399** — estimated biceps brachii
  fiber number in 5 elite bodybuilders, 7 intermediate bodybuilders, and 13 untrained
  controls (needle biopsy + CT). Fiber counts ranged widely between individuals
  (~172,000–419,000), but *despite enormous differences in muscle size, both bodybuilder
  groups had the same fiber number as untrained controls*. The authors concluded heavy
  resistance training aimed at maximum size does not increase fiber number, and that
  people with the biggest muscles simply tend to be born with more fibers.
  https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/jappl.1984.57.5.1399

- The standing physiological consensus is that mammalian fiber number is essentially set
  perinatally. Fiber splitting and new fiber formation *have* been shown in animals (rat,
  cat, chicken) under extreme models — synergist ablation, tenotomy, chronic stretch —
  but have not been demonstrated as a meaningful contributor in adult humans.
  See discussion in Antonio & Gonyea's classic review,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3520748/, and the counter-case for rare hyperplasia at
  the extremes of muscularity in The Physiological Society's
  "Muscle mass at the top: a likely role for fibre hyperplasia in humans,"
  https://www.physoc.org/magazine-articles/muscle-mass-at-the-top-a-likely-role-for-fibre-hyperplasia-in-humans/

**Note:** it is worth being precise here. Those bodybuilders were, in most cases, using
anabolic drugs — and in the modern era, GH among them — and their fiber counts were still
normal. That is about as close to a natural experiment on this question as exists.

## 3. What GH actually does to muscle in controlled human trials

This is the strongest evidence base, and it is consistently unimpressive.

**Young healthy men, GH + resistance training (Yarasheski et al., 1992,
*Am J Physiol* 262:E261)** — 16 men, 21–34 y, 12 weeks of heavy resistance training with
GH or placebo. Both groups gained the same muscle size, the same strength, and the same
muscle protein synthesis rate. GH added fat-free mass, but since it wasn't muscle, the
authors concluded the extra FFM was "lean tissue other than skeletal muscle."
https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/ajpendo.1992.262.3.E261

**Elderly men, GH ± resistance training (Lange et al., 2002, *JCEM* 87(2):513)** —
31 men, age ~74, 12 weeks, four arms (training+placebo, training+GH, GH alone, placebo).
Training raised quadriceps strength, power and cross-sectional area; **adding GH did not
augment any of it.** GH alone shifted myosin heavy chain isoform composition (↑MHC 2X),
i.e. a molecular change, without a strength or size benefit.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11836279/

**Why the lean mass goes up anyway — Doessing et al. (2010), *J Physiol* 588(2):341** —
14 days of rhGH in healthy young people. Tendon collagen I mRNA ↑3.9-fold and tendon
collagen synthesis ↑1.3-fold; muscle collagen I mRNA ↑2.3-fold and muscle collagen
synthesis ↑5.8-fold. **Myofibrillar (contractile) protein synthesis: unchanged.** The
authors' conclusion is the key sentence in this whole literature: GH "is more important in
strengthening the matrix tissue than for muscle cell hypertrophy in adult human
musculotendinous tissue."
https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.2009.179325

**Recreational athletes, RCT (Meinhardt et al., 2010, *Ann Intern Med* 152(9):568)** —
96 trained athletes (63 men, 33 women), mean age 27.9. GH produced significant water
retention and fat loss; endurance, strength, power and muscle mass were not significantly
changed. Where lean body mass rose, it rose **primarily as extracellular water**.
Joint pain and swelling were more common than with placebo.
https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-152-9-201005040-00007

**Systematic review, healthy elderly (Liu et al., 2007, *Ann Intern Med* 146(2):104)** —
pooled trials: ~2 kg lean body mass gained, ~2 kg fat lost, **no improvement in strength,
bone density, lipids, or VO₂max.**
https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-146-2-200701160-00005

**Systematic review, athletic performance (Liu et al., 2008, *Ann Intern Med* 148(10):747)** —
GH increases lean body mass short-term but does not appear to improve strength, and may
worsen exercise capacity.
https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-148-10-200805200-00215

## 4. The cell-level evidence people cite — and what it actually shows

The "new muscle cells" claim usually traces back to genuine findings about **satellite
cells** and **myoblast fusion**. Two things to keep straight:

**(a) Satellite-cell fusion adds nuclei, not fibers.** Mature muscle fibers are
post-mitotic multinucleated syncytia — their nuclei cannot divide. Growth past a certain
point requires new myonuclei donated by satellite cells fusing into the existing fiber.
That is a cell fusing *into* a muscle cell, not a new muscle cell. See
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5986879/

**(b) GH does promote myoblast fusion — in a dish (Sotiropoulos et al., 2006, *PNAS*
103(19):7315).** GH facilitated fusion of myoblasts with nascent myotubes, increasing
myonuclear number, cell-autonomously and **independently of IGF-1 upregulation**, via
NFATc2 (GH could not enlarge NFATc2⁻/⁻ myotubes). Importantly, GH had *no* effect on the
size, proliferation, or differentiation of the myoblast precursors themselves — it acted
at the fusion step on already-differentiating cells.
https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0510033103

That is still not hyperplasia, and it has not carried over to human GH trials. A
counterpoint from the in-vivo literature: 10 weeks of GH treatment produced significant
myofiber hypertrophy *without* increasing nuclei per fiber, lowering the
nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio — the opposite of the myonuclear-accretion story.
Discussed in https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00466.2007

**(c) In animals, most of GH's muscle effect runs through IGF-1.** GH administration
increases muscle weight and myofiber cross-sectional area in wild-type mice, but not in
muscle-specific IGF1R knockouts. Transgenic human-GH overexpression shifts fiber type
(↑ type I). These are fiber *size* and *type* effects, not fiber *number* effects.
Reviewed in Velloso, "Regulation of muscle mass by growth hormone and IGF-I,"
*Br J Pharmacol* 154(3):557,
https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1038/bjp.2008.153

For contrast, the hormone that *does* move satellite cells in humans is testosterone:
Sinha-Hikim et al. found dose-dependent increases in satellite cell number (2.5% → 5.0%
and 15.0% at 300 and 600 mg) and myonuclear number in healthy young men.
https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpendo.00370.2002
No comparable human GH study shows this.

## 5. The natural high-dose experiment: acromegaly

People with acromegaly have decades of pathologically elevated GH. If GH built muscle
cells, they would be the proof.

They have increased lean mass and total body water — and a **myopathy**: weakness, myalgia,
reduced endurance. Biopsies show hypertrophy of type I and type II fibers alongside a
patchy myopathic process, with mixed populations of hypertrophied, atrophied and
normal-sized type II fibers. Bigger, weaker muscle — not more muscle cells.

- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16033731/ (acromegaly as an endocrine myopathy)
- https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7795003/ (GH/IGF-1 abnormalities and muscle impairment)
- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.867921/full (musculoskeletal effects of altered GH action)

## 6. Where GH *is* genuinely anabolic

Worth stating plainly, because "GH does nothing" is also wrong:

- **GH-deficient adults** — replacement restores lean mass, strength and muscle function;
  deficiency alters fiber-type composition. This is correcting a deficit, not enhancement.
- **Catabolic states** — burns, HIV wasting, short bowel syndrome, glucocorticoid excess.
- **Connective tissue** — the collagen effect in Doessing 2010 is large and real, and may
  matter for tendon/matrix integrity. It is also the likeliest reason GH "feels" like it
  is doing something while adding no contractile tissue.
- **Children** — GH drives longitudinal growth, and during development fiber number and
  muscle growth are still plastic in ways they are not in adults.

---

## Bottom line

1. **No human study shows GH increasing muscle fiber number.** Fiber number appears fixed
   after early life, and even elite bodybuilders — drug-using ones — have normal counts.
2. **GH does promote myoblast fusion in vitro and in mice**, which adds *nuclei* to
   existing fibers. That is not a new muscle cell, and it has not been demonstrated with
   GH in humans; at least one in-vivo result points the other way.
3. **In healthy adults, GH does not add contractile muscle protein.** Randomized trials
   show no added strength or hypertrophy over training alone; the lean-mass number goes up
   because of water and collagen.
4. **Chronic GH excess produces big, weak muscle**, which is direct evidence against
   "better/more muscle cells."
5. GH is genuinely useful where there is a deficiency or a catabolic state. As a
   muscle-building drug for a healthy adult, the controlled evidence does not support it.
